The New Democracy: Sample the People and Let Them Choose

By Arda Akgül · Economics Research Union

October 25, 2026 5 min read AI Economies

The Election That Never Happens

Imagine a government planning a major tax reform. Before sending it to parliament, it tests the proposal on five million artificial citizens. It compares different rates, exemptions, and regional effects. The model estimates who benefits, who pays, and where resistance might emerge. Instead of defending one proposal for months, the government can explore a hundred alternatives before choosing one. This remains a hypothetical application, as MatrAIx has not yet been validated for tax-policy forecasting.

This is the part of artificial intelligence in government that genuinely interests me. Should better decisions necessarily require more time and money? We already simulate aircraft, financial markets, and traffic. Could persona-based simulations help us explore more policy alternatives before implementation?

MatrAIx makes this possibility more concrete.

Eight Billion Personas Are Not Eight Billion Voters

MatrAIx, introduced by Li et al. (2026), contains approximately 8.3 billion persona records structured across 1,290 attributes. Its playground lets simulated users answer surveys, interact with chatbots, browse websites, and operate applications. The project also provides 1,010 application tasks across more than 25 domains and reports 18,189 evaluation trials across eight tasks. A quality-filtered set of around one million personas has been released publicly. However, these simulated profiles do not constitute a representative sample of the real-world population, and their predictions still require human validation.

The achievement is infrastructure for repeatable experiments across diverse populations.

Argyle et al. (2023) had already shown that language models can reproduce some survey response patterns. Park et al. (2024) developed agents from interviews with 1,052 people, achieving about 85% of participants' own test-retest consistency on selected questions. MatrAIx pushes this work toward a reusable testing platform.

The reported 91.5% reflects behavioral-attribute adherence in 366 of 400 controlled trials, not election-prediction accuracy. I care about that distinction because it tells us what to improve more. The right response to an imperfect simulation is better validation, better models, and more experiments. Not abandoning the infrastructure.

The Economic Case Nobody Should Ignore

The biggest immediate effect of MatrAIx may be economic rather than electoral. Traditional market research requires recruitment, incentives, fieldwork, and time. Firms often test only a handful of product ideas because every additional experiment has a cost. Synthetic users could make early stage testing dramatically more accessible, especially for smaller companies.

Let’s consider a retailer changing prices. Instead of testing one increase across one sample, it could explore dozens of price points among different income groups and regions. A bank could test whether customers understand a new product. A software company could identify which users abandon an application after an error. None of these experiments guarantees the correct forecast. They do help teams reject weak designs before spending money on a real rollout.

Governments face a larger version of the same problem. A poorly targeted subsidy, a badly designed transit fare, or a confusing public-service application wastes real resources. Simulations could help identify failures early and compare who gains and loses under competing policies. That means less wasted expenditure, faster iteration, and potentially higher productivity in the public sector.

MatrAIx could lower experimentation costs across companies and public institutions. When experimentation becomes cheaper, institutions can test more alternatives. Better allocation is not automatic, but the opportunity is enormous. The paper does not calculate a GDP dividend. The mechanism is nevertheless worth taking seriously.

Prediction Is Not Permission

There is still a distinction I would keep: knowing what I might choose is not the same as having my permission to choose for me.

I call the confusion the proxy-consent fallacy. A digital twin may predict that I support a housing policy. Useful. It can help officials prepare a better proposal. But a simulated preference is not a ballot. Prediction is an input into decision-making; consent is the authority behind a decision.

People also reconsider their political preferences. Bisbee et al. (2024) show that synthetic surveys can reproduce averages while misrepresenting variation. That is a technical challenge we should measure openly, not an excuse to stop developing synthetic populations. Pickering et al. (2026) similarly find that citizens are more receptive to AI assisting elected representatives than replacing them in political decision-making.

How Democracy Gets Quietly Outsourced

The institutional risk is more subtle than government by AI. A ministry first uses synthetic testing to compare ten proposals. Then it consults citizens on only two. Eventually, the simulation becomes the default filter for what reaches public discussion, rather than people themselves.

That is democratic displacement. But the answer is not to slow the models down, it is to make the filtering visible and ensure that simulated approval never becomes a legal substitute for a vote. That sounds like a workable division of labor more than outsourcing it.

The Infrastructure Has an Owner

Who gets to run these experiments matters almost as much as their accuracy. Data, compute, foundation models, and administrative integration are sources of power. Varoufakis (2024) calls attention to concentrated digital infrastructure through his technofeudalism argument. Whatever the label, platform dependence is an economic problem.

Palantir-style systems already show the importance of connecting data, models, and operations (Palantir Technologies, n.d.). The next step is population simulation. If private firms can model consumer behavior while public institutions cannot model policy consequences, governments will negotiate from a weaker position.

I would rather see competing platforms, public research datasets, open evaluations, and governments building their own competence than an artificial shortage of these tools. Build the infrastructure, not another warning label.

Use the Machine, Keep the Citizen

The model I want is straightforward. Simulate policies early. Compare distributional and fiscal effects. Publish assumptions. Test important predictions against real people. Then let elected institutions and citizens decide. The OECD (2020) has shown the value of representative deliberation; synthetic populations could make that deliberation better informed.

For me, technological acceleration and democracy are not opposing objectives. A state that can test more ideas, waste less money, and learn faster is potentially a better state. We should build those capabilities aggressively while keeping political authority where it belongs.

The real danger is not that MatrAIx becomes powerful. It is that only a few institutions become powerful enough to use something like it. I do not want less simulation. I want better simulation, broader access, and the expertise to use it responsibly.

Let the machines test the choices. Let the people make them.
