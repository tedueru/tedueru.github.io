Three-Year Economic Roadmap: The 2027–2029 Medium-Term Program

By Berra Berin Kökkurtöz · Economics Research Union

October 25, 2026 6 min read Macro Policy & MTP

The Medium-Term Program is the government's key document setting macroeconomic targets for the next three years and the assumptions behind them. This article examines the growth, inflation, exchange rate, external balance, and budget projections of the 2027–2029 program.

Growth and Unemployment Projections

Growth for 2026 has been revised down from 3.8% to 3.3%, and the program projects 4.2% for 2027 and 4.6% for 2028. Average growth over the past quarter century is 4.8%, and the program reaches that level only in 2029. Growth therefore stays below its long run average for three years. The unemployment projections move consistently with this path: a decline in the years when growth accelerates and a more limited improvement when growth is lower.

Inflation and Disinflation Trajectory

The program projects year end inflation at 28.4% in 2026, 21% in 2027, 13.5% in 2028, and 9% in 2029. The 2026 estimate is 0.4 percentage points above the Central Bank's latest forecast of 28%, so the starting point is close to the Central Bank's outlook. The main change relative to earlier programs is in the timeline. Single digit inflation, projected for 2027 in the previous program, is now projected for 2029.

The program released two years ago projected 7% inflation for 2027, against 21% in the current one. The disinflation path therefore extends over a longer period. Alongside the growth path, it shows a degree of consistency: growth moves toward its long run average while inflation declines.

Exchange Rates, Deflators, and Real Appreciation

Based on the program's figures, the implied USD/TRY rate is approximately 56 in 2027 and 64 in 2028, compared with approximately 51 and 54 in the previous program. Exchange rate expectations have therefore been revised upward. Even so, over the next three years the GDP deflator is projected to rise by approximately 57% and the exchange rate by approximately 47%.

The lira is thus expected to lose less value against the dollar than domestic prices rise, remaining relatively strong in real terms against domestic prices. In 2027 the exchange rate increase moves closer to inflation than in previous years, and in 2028 and 2029 the two are almost equal (Eğilmez, 2026). Relative to domestic prices, imports stay inexpensive and exports become more expensive.

Over the next three years, the GDP deflator is projected to rise by ~57% while the exchange rate rises by ~47%, implying that the Turkish Lira remains relatively strong in real terms against domestic prices. — Berra Berin Kökkurtöz

Current Account and Foreign Trade Deficits

The program's 2026 current account deficit forecast has risen to USD 47.5 billion, more than twice the previous program's forecast. The exchange rate price relationship above keeps imports relatively cheap and exports relatively expensive, which matters for reading this deficit.

The foreign trade deficit is expected to be around USD 100-110 billion over the period, not very different from previous projections. Changes in exports and imports are generally consistent with the growth projections, except in 2027. In that year, imports change very little although growth accelerates, so the trade deficit stays almost the same. Given the usual relationship between growth and imports, this needs a separate explanation (Eğilmez, 2026).

The 2027 Budget Outlook and Interest Burden

The 2027 budget outlook differs from the other years. The program projects an increase in the budget deficit and a decline in the primary surplus. The increase exceeds what higher interest expenditures alone can explain, because non interest expenditures (personnel, current, and investment spending) are expected to rise faster than inflation.

Interest expenditures are expected to increase by approximately 41% in 2027 compared with 2026, while their growth rate is projected to slow sharply in later years. Such a slowdown could occur if the budget and primary balances improve, and the program anticipates that improvement in subsequent years. However, the projected decline in the deficit does not appear sufficient on its own to explain the sharp slowdown, and the primary balance as a share of GDP does not appear to support it either.

A second explanation is that if the anticipated decline in inflation materializes, interest rates may also fall over time and the growth of interest payments may slow. The decisive factor is therefore whether inflation and interest rates follow the path projected in the program (Eğilmez, 2026).

Output Valuation: Real Growth vs. Price Effects

GDP at current prices measures the total value of goods and services produced in a year at that year's prices. When prices rise rapidly, GDP in lira can increase significantly even if production changes little. Real growth, which removes the price effect, shows how much the economy actually expanded. Real growth in the second quarter of 2026 was 2.3% (TurkStat, 2026).

The program's 2026 figures have the same structure. The annual average GDP deflator is expected to rise by 30.6%, while the exchange rate is expected to rise by only 18.7%. The 11.9 point gap between the two rates contributes to making output look larger in dollar terms. This is not a calculation error but a mathematical consequence of inflation and exchange rate movements. Alongside asking how many billions of dollars GDP has reached, it is therefore important to ask how much of the increase reflects production and how much reflects price and exchange rate movements.

Conclusion: Key Observations on the 2027–2029 MTP

The 2027–2029 MTP presents a framework in which growth, inflation, and unemployment projections are broadly consistent: growth moves toward its long run average by 2029 while inflation declines to 9%. Single digit inflation has moved from 2027 in the previous program to 2029.

Three areas stand out:

  • Domestic prices rise faster than the exchange rate over the program period, maintaining real appreciation pressures.
  • Imports barely change in 2027 despite faster growth, creating a dynamic that warrants close monitoring against trade balances.
  • The 2027 budget deficit widens by more than interest costs explain, indicating fiscal pressures from non-interest expenditures.
